\abstract{Process mining describes behavior through event logs, process trees, and Petri nets. Their structures differ enough that learning methods usually treat them separately. We introduce \model, a behavior-supervised multimodal model that embeds these artifacts in a shared space and translates any supported input into a process tree. Training groups alternative process realizations and log observations into behavior families while distinguishing exact relations from weaker analogies. The quantitative evaluation uses held-out synthetic families drawn from the same generator design as the training data, so it measures in-distribution generalization rather than transfer to real processes. The shared space supports cross-modal retrieval and agrees with an operational behavioral ordering; this is the central result. Grammar-constrained decoding guarantees well-formed, executable candidates, but semantic reconstruction remains weak, particularly from event logs, and specialized discovery gives better conformance. Illustrative real-log cases show the same limitation. Because the output language is restricted to block-structured process trees, it also cannot express every Petri net exactly. \model is therefore most useful as a representation and retrieval layer whose translated candidates require validation. The generator, code, and experimental artifacts support reproduction.}

\keywords{process mining, multimodal representation learning, behavior supervision, process trees, Petri nets, event logs, grammar-constrained decoding}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Process mining uses different artifacts to describe related behavior. Event logs record observed executions. Process trees organize choices, sequences, loops, and parallel work into a readable hierarchy. Petri nets express states, concurrency, and routing directly. These views are connected, but their structures are too different for a representation learned on one view to be used immediately on another.

Figure~\ref{fig:intuitive-example} illustrates the opportunity. A small process can appear as a log, a tree, or a marked net. A shared representation should retrieve the corresponding artifacts across these forms and provide a common starting point for translation. The task is not simple format conversion: a finite log is incomplete, and structurally different models can allow the same observed traces.

\input{figures/intuitive-example}

We introduce \model to learn such a shared representation for event logs, process trees, and Petri nets. Modality-specific encoders map the artifacts into one semantic space, while a grammar-constrained decoder returns a block-structured process tree. Training is organized around \emph{behavior families} that pair alternative model realizations with different log observations and record the strength of their relation.

The evaluation scope is deliberately narrow. Quantitative experiments use held-out synthetic families produced by the same generator design as the training data. They do not establish transfer to industrial event logs. The decoder can return only process trees, so arbitrary non-block-structured Petri nets lie outside its output language. Within this scope, the paper asks:

\begin{description}
\item[\RQone:] Does the shared space align related artifacts and preserve behavioral ordering?
\item[\RQtwo:] How much semantic information is preserved when decoding a valid process tree?
\item[\RQthree:] Does the representation support retrieval, and how does log-to-tree decoding compare with specialized discovery?
\end{description}

The central claim is correspondingly focused. Behavior-family supervision produces a useful cross-artifact representation, whereas constrained decoding guarantees syntax but not semantic fidelity. The paper contributes the family-based supervision scheme, a multimodal architecture, an RQ-aligned evaluation, and released experimental artifacts that expose both outcomes.

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Process representations and discovery}

An event log records completed executions but only partially samples the behavior of an underlying process. A process tree composes activities and silent steps through sequence, exclusive choice, parallelism, and loop operators. Its block structure permits conversion into sound workflow nets. A Petri net expresses places, transitions, arcs, and markings directly and can represent non-block structures not expressible by the chosen tree grammar \citep{vanDerAalst2016,Kourani2026}.

Classical process discovery constructs a model from a log. Inductive Miner recursively detects cuts and returns a sound block-structured result \citep{Leemans2013}. We use the PM4Py implementation for conversion, simulation, and evaluation \citep{Berti2023PM4Py}. Learned discovery instead estimates a model from examples; graph-neural discovery is one relevant direction \citep{Sommers2023}. Our task is broader than discovery: the same parameters encode three artifact types, support retrieval and geometry, and decode all of them into one exchange language. Inductive Miner is therefore the relevant specialized discovery baseline.

Conformance combines several perspectives. Fitness measures whether observed traces can be replayed, while precision penalizes model behavior unsupported by the log \citep{Adriansyah2013}. Directly-follows summaries are computationally convenient but lose long-range and concurrency information \citep{vanDerAalst2019DFG}. We therefore report both distributional behavioral distances and process-model conformance, and avoid treating any single metric as semantics in full.

\subsection{Representation learning for process mining}

Trace-encoding methods range from handcrafted features to sequence models; their assumptions and performance vary considerably by task \citep{Barbon2023}. Earlier work learned representations at activity, trace, log, and model levels \citep{DeKoninck2018}. Petri-net embeddings have also been learned from graph structure \citep{Colonna2024}, and graph distances can be compared with model-similarity measures \citep{Dijkman2011}. These approaches establish the value of vector representations but generally do not align raw logs, hierarchical trees, and marked Petri graphs in one behavior-supervised space.

A concurrent direction uses in-context learning on event logs for predictive monitoring \citep{Berti2026ICFM}. That setting centers on one input modality and prediction over cases. \model instead aligns multiple artifact types and produces complete structured models. The two directions are complementary: a future system could combine event-level monitoring capabilities with artifact-level semantic translation.

\subsection{Multimodal alignment and constrained generation}

Contrastive multimodal learning demonstrates that paired observations can define a shared space without requiring identical encoders \citep{Radford2021}. Supervised contrastive objectives allow all members of a semantic class to act as positives \citep{Khosla2020}. Our classes are behavior families rather than image--text pairs, and the recorded relation strength controls which pairs may be used as exact positives. Variance and covariance regularization reduce collapse without imposing a probabilistic prior \citep{Bardes2022}.

Transformers provide the attention mechanism used in the process-tree encoder, set-attention component, and decoder \citep{Vaswani2017}. The event-log encoder must additionally be invariant to trace order, motivating set attention \citep{Lee2019}. For output, grammar-constrained decoding can guarantee syntactic structure even when model probabilities are imperfect \citep{Geng2023}. Our decoder extends this principle with explicit operator arities, source-activity masking, contextual copy scores, and deterministic completion when the search budget is exhausted.

\section{Problem formulation and research questions}\label{sec:setting}

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a finite activity alphabet and $\tauact$ an invisible activity. A trace is a word $\sigma\in\mathcal{A}^{*}$, and an event log $L$ is a finite multiset of traces. A process tree $T$ is generated by
\begin{equation}
T ::= a \mid \tauact \mid \mathsf{SEQ}_{k}(T_1,\ldots,T_k)
 \mid \mathsf{XOR}_{k}(T_1,\ldots,T_k)
 \mid \mathsf{AND}_{k}(T_1,\ldots,T_k)
 \mid \mathsf{LOOP}(T_1,T_2),
\label{eq:grammar}
\end{equation}
where $a\in\mathcal{A}$ and $k\geq2$. A labeled Petri net is a tuple $N=(P,U,F,\ell,m_0,m_f)$ with places $P$, transitions $U$, flow relation $F$, labels $\ell:U\rightarrow\mathcal{A}\cup\{\tauact\}$, and initial and final markings $m_0,m_f$. Petri nets can express non-block-structured behavior that the tree grammar cannot.

A \emph{behavior family} $f$ contains a canonical tree $T_f$, two Petri-net realizations $N_f^{(1)},N_f^{(2)}$, and two event-log observations $L_f^{(u)},L_f^{(r)}$. Their pairing is
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & L_f^{(u)} & L_f^{(r)}\\
\hline
N_f^{(1)} & (T_f,N_f^{(1)},L_f^{(u)}) & (T_f,N_f^{(1)},L_f^{(r)})\\
N_f^{(2)} & (T_f,N_f^{(2)},L_f^{(u)}) & (T_f,N_f^{(2)},L_f^{(r)})
\end{array}
\]
Thus one family produces four aligned rows. The same underlying tree is paired with every row; each log observation and Petri-net realization participates in two rows.

For modality $m\in\{T,L,N\}$, an encoder returns source memory $H_m$ and a normalized semantic vector $\vect z_m$. The shared-embedding task places related family members near one another despite their different structures. Cross-modal retrieval ranks candidate artifacts by cosine similarity between these vectors. Direct translation conditions a decoder on $H_m$ and returns a tree in Eq.~\eqref{eq:grammar}. For optional fusion, the available semantic vectors are averaged,
\begin{equation}
\vect z_{\mathrm{fuse}}=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{m\in S}\vect z_m,
\qquad S\subseteq\{T,L,N\},
\label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
and projected into decoder memory. Translation is therefore always into a process tree, even when the source is a Petri net. These tasks instantiate \RQone--\RQthree from the Introduction.

\section{The ProcRosetta method}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Behavior-family supervision}\label{sec:corpus}

Figure~\ref{fig:corpus} summarizes corpus construction. The generator follows four conceptual stages:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Generate an ordinary random process tree or insert a controlled motif.
\item Construct two process-model realizations of the resulting process.
\item Establish the relation between the realizations and generate two clean event-log observations.
\item Canonicalize activity labels and pair the model and log views into four aligned rows.
\end{enumerate}

Ordinary families pair a tree-derived workflow net with an isomorphically renamed realization. Controlled families introduce the duplicate-prefix, concurrency/interleaving, and non-free-choice M-pattern contrasts in Fig.~\ref{fig:motifs}. The same activity notation is used across all panels. The controlled fragments are placed inside randomized contexts so that the model cannot succeed by recognizing a single fixed template.

Each family supplies two clean log observations derived from a common trace pool: a coverage-oriented view and an independently resampled view. No corruption is stored in the evaluated corpus. Incomplete or noisy presentations occur only as training-time augmentation. For each log view, a first-seen map converts activities to $A_0,A_1,\ldots$ and is applied to that view's tree, log, and Petri net. This removes arbitrary source names as a shortcut while preserving a mapping back to external labels.

\subsection{Generator implementation and relation checks}\label{sec:generation}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:generation} makes the data-generation procedure explicit. A global seed is expanded with BLAKE2b into family-specific streams for the process model, representation, playout, log view, and augmentation. Structural sampling is therefore independent from observation sampling. Ordinary topologies are retried until the requested profile constraints are met, with a limit of 4,000 attempts.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Behavior-family generation}
\label{alg:generation}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require curriculum profile $c$, family target $n$, deterministic seed $s$
\State initialize split-local family list $\mathcal{F}\leftarrow\varnothing$
\State allocate motif and ordinary-root quotas
\While{$|\mathcal{F}|<n$}
  \State derive family-specific random streams from $s$
  \State sample an ordinary tree or place a controlled motif in a shared context
  \State construct paired Petri-net realizations $N^{(1)},N^{(2)}$
  \State reject failed conversion, reachability, or dead-transition checks
  \If{$T$ is an ordinary looping tree}
    \State establish the paired construction as isomorphic
    \State simulate seeded tree playouts to form a clean trace pool
  \Else
    \State enumerate each visible language under the configured safeguards
    \State reject empty or unequal collected languages
    \State record exact evidence if both explorations exhaust; bounded evidence otherwise
  \EndIf
  \State derive coverage-oriented and resampled clean log observations
  \State canonicalize each view and flatten two model variants by two log views
  \State store relation metadata, targets, seeds, and four rows
\EndWhile
\State verify coverage constraints and commit the split
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

For non-looping families, marking exploration stops after 5,000 visited states, 10,000 completed traces, visible length 32, or the silent-transition firing safeguard. Exhausting both state spaces establishes exact visible-language evidence; reaching a limit establishes only a bounded comparison, which is never used as an exact positive. For ordinary looping pairs, exact supervision instead follows from the construction-level isomorphism. Normalized language hashes and partial-order identifiers record these distinctions without extending the claim beyond the available certificate.

The generator rejects unsound conversions, unreachable final markings, and dead transitions before storing a family. It also verifies motif coverage and split-local identifiers after generation. The resulting certificates are part of each row rather than reconstructed later from surface similarity.

The shared decoder predicts tree structure, operator arity, and visible activities. Its copy component can reuse activities represented in source memory. During generation, a small automaton tracks open child slots and masks illegal tokens. End-of-sequence is available only when every slot is filled; if the search budget is exhausted, deterministic completion closes the remaining slots. The decoder therefore guarantees a well-formed tree within the supported grammar, but this guarantee says nothing about behavioral accuracy. The evaluated synthetic policy restricts activities to the source alphabet and suppresses repeated visible labels. Because repeated labels can be legitimate, the external-log cases also evaluate the same latent with that mask disabled.

\subsection{Training objectives and checkpoint selection}\label{sec:training}

Training combines three groups of objectives:

\begin{description}
\item[Reconstruction.] Single-modality and fused representations are trained to generate the canonical process tree.
\item[Alignment.] Exact-pair contrast, relation hierarchy, alternate-observation consistency, and operational behavioral geometry organize the shared space.
\item[Regularization.] Variance, covariance, termination, length, and structural-completion terms discourage collapse and unstable generation.
\end{description}

The complete objective is
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}={}&
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}
 +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fuse}}
 +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{exact}}
 +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hier}}
 +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{obs}}
 +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{geom}}
 +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}.
\label{eq:objective}
\end{align}
Table~\ref{tab:loss-weights} resolves these groups into the actual checkpoint coefficients. A zero Kullback--Leibler weight is consistent with the deterministic semantic latent: the model does not sample from a learned latent distribution.

\subsection{Fixed evaluation subset}

We evaluate 16 families from each of the three complexity levels, for 48 families in total; each family contributes four aligned rows (192 rows and 768 decoding runs across four source conditions). Families are chosen deterministically with interleaving across complexity, motif, and observation strata. Results refer to this subset unless explicitly described as corpus statistics.

For decoded-model comparisons, tree-edit distance is normalized by the larger folded-tree size. The operational behavioral distance between families is
\begin{equation}
d_B(f,g)=\frac{1}{3}\left(
 \lVert p_{\mathrm{var}}^f-p_{\mathrm{var}}^g\rVert_1+
 \lVert p_{\mathrm{df}}^f-p_{\mathrm{df}}^g\rVert_1+
 \lVert p_{\mathrm{len}}^f-p_{\mathrm{len}}^g\rVert_1
\right),
\label{eq:behavior-distance}
\end{equation}
where the terms compare trace-variant, boundary-aware directly-follows, and trace-length distributions. This metric overlaps with statistics used by the training-time behavioral descriptor. Agreement with $d_B$ is therefore evidence about the supervised operational notion of behavior, not a general process-semantic metric.

Uncertainty is estimated by resampling complete families, never individual rows, with replacement \citep{Efron1994}. Percentile intervals summarize variation within the fixed subset; they do not include generator, training-seed, or checkpoint uncertainty.

Behavioral averages are conditional on successful evaluation, so coverage appears in the results table.

For \RQthree, the row-level retrieval gallery retains the balanced expansion: the underlying tree contributes four instances, while each log observation and Petri-net realization contributes two. View-specific activity maps can make repeated instances surface-different. All four rows of the matching family are relevant. Equal multiplicity leaves the random family prior unchanged, but multiple positive views can make this protocol easier than a deduplicated gallery; the claim is therefore limited to the stated row-level setting. MRR can also depend on deterministic tie order and is treated as secondary. The discovery comparison evaluates log-decoded trees and Inductive Miner using footprint fitness and precision.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{\RQone: Why multimodal alignment succeeds}

Behavior families provide a supervision unit above surface syntax. Alternate event-log observations teach invariance to sampling, while paired Petri-net realizations teach invariance to representation. Separating exact, isomorphic, and analogue relations prevents a useful structural contrast from becoming a false identity. The result is a space that can support neighborhoods and retrieval even when the encoders share no input format.

The behavioral-ordering result is narrower. Its target is an operational descriptor related to training supervision, and deterministic features remain competitive. The contribution is therefore the availability of one learned interface from every modality, not the replacement of all modality-specific similarity measures.

\subsection{\RQtwo: Why semantic decoding remains difficult}

Alignment requires related artifacts to occupy compatible neighborhoods; decoding requires a long discrete structure to be recovered from an incomplete or compressed representation. A finite event log can support several plausible trees, while training designates one canonical target. An early structural error also changes all later open slots in prefix generation, so local mistakes propagate.

The copy mechanism controls the activity vocabulary, and the grammar controls well-formedness, but neither chooses the correct control-flow placement. Better generation will require objectives that score complete behavior, stronger size priors, or conformance-aware reranking. A richer output language would also be needed for Petri nets that have no equivalent block-structured tree.

The duplicate-label comparison isolates one possible source of error. Disabling the mask does not change the top candidate for either real log, so their low conformance cannot be attributed to forced label uniqueness. In these cases the decoder instead omits activities and compresses control flow too aggressively.

\subsection{\RQthree: Appropriate and inappropriate uses}

The current model is appropriate for encoding heterogeneous artifacts, retrieving related material, comparing neighborhoods, and proposing process-tree candidates for validation. Direct translation uses source memory, retrieval uses semantic vectors, and exploratory fusion combines semantic vectors before decoding. These uses are available through the command-line and browser interfaces; Section~\ref{sec:reproduction} gives the operational commands used in the paper.

The model is not an unattended replacement for process discovery or conformance checking. A practical workflow should retrieve context or propose candidates with \model, then validate behavior with established process-mining methods. Fusion is a transparent common interface but does not yet improve reconstruction, suggesting that future fusion should account for source reliability.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Limitations}\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Data and generalization.}
The limitation is that training and inferential evaluation use synthetic families from the same generator design, without timestamps, resources, lifecycle attributes, drift, or label semantics. The real-log evidence covers only two available logs, one of them a small extract, and neither comes with aligned reference trees and Petri nets. Families are split without row leakage, but the synthetic splits are not guaranteed to be behavior-disjoint, and the process-tree output language cannot express every Petri net. Consequently, the results do not establish industrial transfer or general Petri-net translation. The appropriate mitigation is a multi-artifact benchmark with real processes, generator shift, global behavior deduplication, and explicit coverage of non-block structures.

\paragraph{Evaluation and uncertainty.}
The limitation is a fixed subset, one training seed, finite behavioral sampling, and conditional evaluability for some large outputs. In addition, $d_B$ overlaps the training descriptor. Consequently, the intervals describe family variation inside this experiment rather than training or domain uncertainty, and geometry should not be read as general semantic equivalence. Larger family-complete evaluations, repeated training runs, independent conformance measures, and explicit failure accounting are needed.

\paragraph{Comparative evidence.}
The limitation is that the study compares with deterministic representation baselines and Inductive Miner but does not include every discovery method, a directly comparable learned multimodal system, or retrained component ablations. Software-version sensitivity also affects simulation and conformance. Consequently, the experiment establishes system-level behavior but not the causal necessity of every architecture or loss term. Retrained ablations, broader baselines, locked environments, and independent replications would strengthen the evidence.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\model aligns event logs, process trees, and Petri nets through behavior-family supervision and a shared semantic interface. The representation supports cross-artifact retrieval and operational behavioral ordering, while grammar-constrained translation remains semantically unreliable despite guaranteed syntax. Illustrative real-log cases confirm the discovery gap and show that allowing repeated labels alone does not repair it. The model is therefore best used to retrieve related artifacts and propose candidates that are subsequently validated. The most important next experiment is evaluation on a behavior-disjoint multi-artifact benchmark containing real processes and structures outside the process-tree grammar.
