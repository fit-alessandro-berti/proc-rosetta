\begin{abstract}
Process mining revolves around heterogeneous artifacts: event logs record observed behavior, while process trees and Petri nets specify intended or discovered behavior. Every established technique that connects two of these artifact types---process discovery, conformance checking, model transformation---is a dedicated algorithm for one direction of one artifact pair. This paper presents \emph{ProcRosetta}, a multimodal neural framework that embeds event logs, process trees, and Petri nets into a single shared latent space of process behavior and decodes any point of that space into a syntactically valid process tree. Three artifact-specific encoders (a recurrent tree encoder, a hierarchical attention-based log encoder, and a message-passing Petri-net encoder) are trained jointly with a shared grammar-masked decoder on synthetic paired triples, using reconstruction, cross-modal translation, latent alignment, contrastive, and variational objectives; grammar masking and deterministic tree-to-net conversion make every generated model well-formed by construction. On a held-out test split of 1{,}024 process triples grouped into 512 behavior families, ProcRosetta decodes valid, Petri-convertible process trees from all three modalities in 100\% of the cases, translates unseen event logs into process models that reach an alignment-based F1 of 0.938 (compared to 0.980 for the Inductive Miner), retrieves exact cross-modal counterparts above chance level, and produces 256-dimensional embeddings whose geometry correlates with behavioral distance on par with much higher-dimensional deterministic log representations. The implementation, data generator, and evaluation suite are publicly available.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Process mining connects observed event data with formal process models~\cite{vanDerAalst2016} and constantly moves between different \emph{representations} of the same behavior: an event log is a finite sample of executed traces, a process tree is a block-structured hierarchical specification, and a Petri net is a graph-based execution model with explicit states and concurrency. The bridges between these views are dedicated, hand-designed algorithms, each for one direction of one artifact pair: discovery maps logs to models~\cite{Aalst2004,Leemans2013,Augusto2019Split}, conformance checking relates logs and models through alignments~\cite{Adriansyah2012}, and model-to-model transformations connect notations such as workflow nets and process trees~\cite{VanZelst2020}. What is missing is a common \emph{computational representation} in which a log, a process tree, and a Petri net describing the same behavior are simply \emph{the same point}. Such a representation would enable similarity search and clustering across artifact types, provide fixed-length behavioral feature vectors for downstream machine learning, and turn translation between artifact types into a decode operation. In machine learning, embedding heterogeneous modalities such as images and text into one shared vector space has proven remarkably effective~\cite{Radford2021,Baltrusaitis2019}; process mining has adopted representation learning for traces and logs~\cite{DeKoninck2018,Barbon2023} and for Petri nets~\cite{Colonna2024}, but these efforts remain \emph{single-modality}, and none of them can generate a process model back from the learned space.

This paper presents \textbf{ProcRosetta}, a multimodal neural framework that treats event logs, process trees, and Petri nets as three views of one process behavior. ProcRosetta trains three artifact-specific encoders into a shared latent space, aligns the views of the same process through explicit alignment and contrastive objectives, and attaches a single shared decoder that maps any latent vector back to a process tree. The decoder is \emph{grammar-masked}: at every generation step, only tokens that keep the partial output a syntactically valid process tree are allowed, following the philosophy of grammar-constrained decoding~\cite{Kusner2017,Geng2023}; decoded trees are converted to Petri nets deterministically using PM4Py~\cite{Berti2023PM4Py}, so every generated model is well-formed and sound by construction. The name is an analogy to the Rosetta stone: the training data is the same content in three ``languages'', and the model learns the correspondence.

Figure~\ref{fig:outline} summarizes the framework. Three research questions organize the investigation:

\begin{itemize}
  \item[\textbf{RQ1}] Can event logs, process trees, and Petri nets be embedded into a shared latent space in which the three views of the same process behavior receive nearby representations?
  \item[\textbf{RQ2}] Can a learned model translate event logs or Petri nets into \emph{valid} process trees---well-formed, Petri-convertible models whose behavior is close to the source?
  \item[\textbf{RQ3}] How do the learned representations and the learned log-to-model translation compare with classical baselines: deterministic log and model features, Petri-net embeddings, and the Inductive Miner?
\end{itemize}

The contributions are: (i)~a multimodal architecture with three artifact-specific encoders projecting into one latent space of process behavior; (ii)~a shared, grammar-masked process-tree decoder that guarantees syntactic validity of every generated model and, through deterministic conversion, yields sound Petri nets; (iii)~a training procedure over synthetic paired triples combining reconstruction, cross-modal translation, latent alignment, contrastive, and variational objectives; and (iv)~a comprehensive assessment with a publicly available implementation, covering decode quality, discovery quality against the Inductive Miner, behavior-aligned embedding quality, and cross-modal retrieval.

Section~\ref{sec:relatedWork} reviews related work; Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries} introduces the process-mining objects; Section~\ref{sec:approach} presents the framework; Section~\ref{sec:tool} presents the interactive tool support; Section~\ref{sec:assessment} reports the assessment; Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} discuss and conclude.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedWork}

\paragraph{Process discovery and block-structured models.}
Automated process discovery derives a process model from an event log~\cite{Aalst2004}; a large benchmark over real-life logs showed that no single algorithm dominates across fitness, precision, generalization, and simplicity~\cite{Augusto2019Review}. The Inductive Miner~\cite{Leemans2013} discovers \emph{process trees} by recursive log decomposition, yielding block-structured models that are sound by construction; process trees are a standard pivot representation with formal conversions to Petri nets and BPMN~\cite{Leemans2026}, and workflow nets can be translated back to process trees when possible~\cite{VanZelst2020}. Beyond strict block structure, Split Miner~\cite{Augusto2019Split} balances accuracy and simplicity, and the POWL family~\cite{Kourani2023POWL,Kourani2026} discovers partially ordered workflow models that are more expressive than process trees while preserving soundness. ProcRosetta shares the Inductive Miner's process-tree bias---the decoder emits process trees because their grammar guarantees well-formedness and their Petri-net conversion is deterministic---but instead of a deterministic log-to-model algorithm, it learns a shared representation over logs, trees, and nets from which models can be decoded.

\paragraph{Model similarity, conformance, and behavioral distances.}
Similarity of process models has been measured over labels, structure, and behavior~\cite{Dijkman2011}, with the recurring insight that behavioral closeness cannot be reduced to structural resemblance; alignment-based conformance checking~\cite{Adriansyah2012} provides exact, interpretable comparisons between logs and models. ProcRosetta learns a \emph{continuous proxy} for such comparisons---attractive for scalable retrieval and clustering, but without formal guarantees---so the assessment measures explicitly how well the learned geometry agrees with a behavioral distance (Section~\ref{sec:embeddingresults}).

\paragraph{Representation learning for process-mining artifacts.}
Representation learning entered process mining chiefly through single-modality embeddings: act2vec, trace2vec, log2vec, and model2vec~\cite{DeKoninck2018} and a rich design space of trace encodings~\cite{Barbon2023}. Deep sequence models for predictive monitoring~\cite{RamaManeiro2023} learn powerful trace representations but target prediction over running cases rather than model generation or cross-artifact alignment. On the model side, Petri nets are graphs, making random-walk embeddings such as node2vec~\cite{Grover2016} and graph neural networks~\cite{Kipf2017} relevant. Most directly related is PetriNet2Vec by Colonna et al.~\cite{Colonna2024}, which learns unsupervised vector representations of Petri nets and serves as a baseline in our assessment. All of these approaches embed \emph{one} artifact family; ProcRosetta's distinctive step is to align three artifact families in one space and to couple the space with a generative decoder.

\paragraph{Neural and LLM-based process-model generation.}
Sommers et al.~\cite{Sommers2021} train graph neural networks on synthetic log--model pairs to discover sound Petri nets; this is the closest neural predecessor, though it addresses one direction (log$\to$net) rather than a tri-modal space. More recently, large language models have been used to generate process models from natural-language descriptions~\cite{Kourani2024LLM}, also specialized via reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards~\cite{Berti2025RL}. These approaches are language-native, whereas ProcRosetta is artifact-native---its inputs are logs, trees, and nets themselves---so the directions are complementary.

\paragraph{Multimodal learning and grammar-constrained decoding.}
Outside process mining, multimodal learning studies shared representations across modalities~\cite{Baltrusaitis2019}; CLIP~\cite{Radford2021} demonstrated contrastive alignment of paired images and captions at scale. ProcRosetta transfers this recipe to process mining, with the twist that its ``modalities'' are formal artifacts whose equivalence is behavioral rather than perceptual. For generation, grammar-constrained decoding guarantees syntactic validity of structured outputs, as in grammar variational autoencoders~\cite{Kusner2017} and grammar-constrained LLM decoding~\cite{Geng2023}; ProcRosetta applies this principle to process trees, combining ideas that have so far been separate---process-tree soundness, graph encoders for Petri nets, log encoders, contrastive multimodal alignment, and grammar-constrained decoding---in a single trainable framework.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

We fix a finite alphabet of activity labels $\Sigma$. Throughout the paper, activity labels are \emph{canonicalized} to $\mathrm{A0}, \mathrm{A1}, \ldots$ in order of first occurrence, making all representations invariant to arbitrary activity naming while preserving repeated-activity identity.

\paragraph{Event logs.}
A \emph{trace} is a finite word $\sigma = \langle a_1, \ldots, a_m \rangle$ with $a_j \in \Sigma$; an \emph{event log} is a finite multiset of traces, $L = [\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_n] \in \mathcal{B}(\Sigma^*)$, preserving trace frequencies. Logs carry only control-flow information here, the classical control-flow abstraction of richer formats such as XES~\cite{IEEE1849-2023}.

\paragraph{Process trees.}
A \emph{process tree}~\cite{Leemans2013,Leemans2026} is a block-structured process model generated by the grammar
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
T \;::=\;& a \;\mid\; \tau \;\mid\; \operatorname{SEQ}(T_1,\ldots,T_k) \;\mid\; \operatorname{XOR}(T_1,\ldots,T_k)\\
&\mid\; \operatorname{AND}(T_1,\ldots,T_k) \;\mid\; \operatorname{LOOP}(T_{\mathit{body}},T_{\mathit{redo}})
\end{split}
\label{eq:grammar}
\end{equation}
with $k \ge 2$, where $a \in \Sigma$ is a visible activity, $\tau$ is a silent step, $\operatorname{SEQ}$ executes its children in order, $\operatorname{XOR}$ selects exactly one child, $\operatorname{AND}$ interleaves its children, and $\operatorname{LOOP}$ repeats a body through a redo branch. Every process tree can be converted into a sound workflow net~\cite{Leemans2013}. Since $\operatorname{XOR}$ and $\operatorname{AND}$ are commutative, their children are sorted by a canonical structural key, giving behaviorally identical permutations one canonical representation.

\paragraph{Petri nets.}
An \emph{accepting labeled Petri net}~\cite{vanDerAalst2016} is a tuple $\mathit{PN} = (P, T, F, \ell, M_0, M_f)$ with places $P$, transitions $T$ ($P \cap T = \emptyset$), flow relation $F \subseteq (P \times T) \cup (T \times P)$, labeling $\ell : T \to \Sigma \cup \{\tau\}$, and initial and final markings $M_0, M_f$. For neural encoding, a Petri net is represented as a \emph{typed graph}: each node carries a type (place, visible transition, or invisible transition) and its token counts in $M_0$ and $M_f$; each arc carries a direction type. Nodes are sorted by name before tensorization, making the representation deterministic.

\section{The ProcRosetta Framework}
\label{sec:approach}

This section presents the paired training data, the encoders, the grammar-masked decoder, and the training objective, briefly explaining the neural building blocks where they appear.

\subsection{Synthetic Paired Triples}
\label{sec:datagen}

ProcRosetta is trained on synthetic \emph{paired triples} whose correspondence is fixed by the generator (Figure~\ref{fig:triple}): each sample $x_i = (T_i, L_i, G_i, e_i)$ belongs to one behavior family $e_i$ with a canonical process tree $T_i$.
\begin{itemize}
  \item canonical ordinary trees are sampled from $\{\operatorname{SEQ}, \operatorname{XOR}, \operatorname{AND}, \operatorname{LOOP}\}$ trees (maximum recursion depth $8$, arity $2$--$3$ for non-loop operators, two-child loops, at most $30$ activities, reuse probability $0.15$) and canonicalized (Section~\ref{sec:preliminaries});
  \item each behavior is stored in two rows with the same tree target and the same visible traces, but with different Petri-net graphs; the second graph may simply rename nodes or use different internal routing while preserving the same behavior;
  \item $L_i$ is a $128$-trace log view sampled from the family's visible behavior, and $G_i$ is the typed-graph representation of the corresponding Petri-net variant.
\end{itemize}
The generator uses a fixed random seed and produces three disjoint family-wise splits: $8{,}192$ training, $1{,}024$ validation, and $1{,}024$ test rows, corresponding to $4{,}096$, $512$, and $512$ behavior families (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). Loops are enabled in the ordinary-tree family. Since loop languages are generally unbounded, ordinary loop families use process-tree playout to construct the finite observed trace pool, while their alternate Petri-net representation is certified by isomorphic renaming. The controlled duplicate-label, concurrency, and non-free-choice families remain checked by bounded complete visible-language enumeration. After regenerating the full splits and rerunning training, aggregate statistics and empirical result tables should be refreshed from the new metadata and test report.

For neural processing, each artifact is \emph{tokenized}. A process tree becomes a prefix sequence over a $40$-token vocabulary (special tokens, operators, arities, and activities $\mathrm{A0}, \ldots, \mathrm{A29}$), where each operator is followed by an explicit arity token and then its children (Figure~\ref{fig:triple}b). Traces are sequences over the $31$-token activity vocabulary. The Petri net graph is tensorized into node-type indices, marking features, and two directed adjacency matrices (one per arc direction).

\subsection{Three Encoders, One Latent Space}
\label{sec:encoders}

Each encoder maps its artifact to a diagonal Gaussian $q_m(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu_m, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_m^2))$ over a shared $D_z$-dimensional latent space ($D_z = 256$), for modality $m \in \{\mathit{tree}, \mathit{trace}, \mathit{petri}\}$, as in variational autoencoders~\cite{KingmaWelling2014}: $\mu_m$ is the embedding of the artifact and $\sigma_m$ the encoder's uncertainty. During training, latent vectors are sampled as $z_m = \mu_m + \epsilon \odot \sigma_m$ with $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, which regularizes the space; at test time the deterministic mean $z_m = \mu_m$ is used.

\emph{Tree encoder.} The token sequence of the process tree is embedded and processed by a gated recurrent unit (GRU)---a standard recurrent network that reads a sequence left to right while maintaining a hidden state---with hidden size $H = 96$; the hidden state at the last non-padding token is projected linearly to $(\mu_{\mathit{tree}}, \sigma_{\mathit{tree}})$.

\emph{Log encoder.} The event-log encoder is \emph{hierarchical}, mirroring the log/trace/event structure: a GRU first encodes each trace into a trace vector; an attention mechanism then computes a data-dependent weight for every trace---emphasizing informative trace variants rather than averaging uniformly---and pools the trace vectors into a single log vector, projected to $(\mu_{\mathit{trace}}, \sigma_{\mathit{trace}})$.

\emph{Petri-net encoder.} The Petri-net encoder is a message-passing graph network~\cite{Kipf2017}. Each node starts from an embedding of its type (place / visible transition / invisible transition), its visible transition label when present, and a linear encoding of its marking feature; in each of three message-passing rounds, every node updates its vector from its own state and its incoming and outgoing neighbors, followed by a nonlinearity and layer normalization. Mean-pooling over nodes yields the graph vector, projected to $(\mu_{\mathit{petri}}, \sigma_{\mathit{petri}})$.

When several views are available, ProcRosetta also defines a \emph{fused} embedding, the mean $\mu_{\mathit{fused}} = \tfrac{1}{3}(\mu_{\mathit{tree}} + \mu_{\mathit{trace}} + \mu_{\mathit{petri}})$.

\subsection{Grammar-Masked Process-Tree Decoder}
\label{sec:decoder}

A single decoder, shared by all three modalities, is an autoregressive GRU: the latent vector $z$ initializes its hidden state, and tokens are generated one at a time, each step predicting a distribution over the vocabulary given the tokens so far.

An unconstrained decoder could produce malformed sequences (e.g., an operator without children). ProcRosetta therefore applies a \emph{grammar mask} derived from a prefix grammar of process trees. The decoding state tracks the number of open node slots and whether an arity token is pending: after $\texttt{<bos>}$, exactly one node is open; an activity or $\tau$ closes one slot; an operator token must be followed by an arity token $\mathrm{ARITY}\_k$, which opens $k$ child slots; when no slots remain open, only $\texttt{<eos>}$ is valid. At every step, the logits (pre-softmax scores) of all grammar-invalid tokens are set to $-10^9$, so invalid continuations receive zero probability during training and greedy decoding. Consequently, \emph{every} terminated output parses into a process tree of grammar~(\ref{eq:grammar}), and PM4Py's deterministic conversion turns it into a sound Petri net---transferring the validity-by-construction philosophy of grammar-based molecular and program generation~\cite{Kusner2017,Geng2023} to process models.

\subsection{Training Objective}
\label{sec:training}

Training uses \emph{teacher forcing}: the decoder receives the ground-truth token sequence shifted by one position and learns to predict every next token. Since all three artifacts of a triple share the same target tree, each mini-batch trains three decoding pathways through the same decoder---reconstruction ($z_{\mathit{tree}} \to T$) and two cross-modal translations ($z_{\mathit{trace}} \to T$, $z_{\mathit{petri}} \to T$)---via grammar-masked sequence cross-entropies ($\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{tree}\to\mathit{tree}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{trace}\to\mathit{tree}}$, $\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{petri}\to\mathit{tree}}$) with label smoothing $0.08$ distributed only over grammar-valid tokens.

Two objectives shape the latent space. The \emph{alignment loss} penalizes disagreement between the latent means of the same sample,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{align}} = \tfrac{1}{3}\big( \lVert\mu_{\mathit{tree}}-\mu_{\mathit{trace}}\rVert_2^2 + \lVert\mu_{\mathit{tree}}-\mu_{\mathit{petri}}\rVert_2^2 + \lVert\mu_{\mathit{trace}}-\mu_{\mathit{petri}}\rVert_2^2 \big),
\end{equation}
pulling the three views of one process toward the same point. The \emph{contrastive loss}, in the style of CLIP~\cite{Radford2021}, additionally pushes \emph{different} processes apart: for every modality pair within a mini-batch, a symmetric multi-positive InfoNCE loss over cosine similarities (temperature $0.2$) rewards every row with the same behavior-family identifier as a positive. Finally, a weak KL regularizer $\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{KL}}$ toward a standard normal prior~\cite{KingmaWelling2014} keeps the latent space bounded and smooth. The total loss is the weighted sum
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \underbrace{\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{tree}\to\mathit{tree}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathit{trace}\to\mathit{tree}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathit{petri}\to\mathit{tree}}}_{\text{reconstruction and translation}} + \; 0.1\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{align}} + 0.1\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{contrastive}} + 0.001\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathit{KL}}.
\label{eq:loss}
\end{equation}

Table~\ref{tab:hyper} summarizes the configuration. The model is deliberately small ($\approx$0.3M parameters), with standard safeguards against overfitting: dropout, weight decay, label smoothing, stochastic latent sampling, family-grouped positives, activity remapping, validation-based learning-rate reduction, early stopping, and best-validation checkpoint retention.

\section{Interactive Tool Support}
\label{sec:tool}

ProcRosetta is accompanied by an interactive Streamlit tool that turns the framework into an inspectable workflow. Users can collect event logs, process trees, and Petri nets in a common workspace, encode individual or grouped artifacts, and translate their representations back into process trees. The translation view presents the generated tree, its validity checks, the deterministically derived Petri net, and a comparison with the source artifact. A complementary latent-space view supports projections, similarity and group-agreement inspection, nearest-neighbor exploration, and interpolation. The tool also provides checkpoint summaries, guided evaluation, and export of embeddings and generated models. Figure~\ref{fig:tool} illustrates the translation and latent-space views.

\section{Assessment}
\label{sec:assessment}

The assessment follows the three research questions, covering training behavior, decode quality (RQ2), discovery quality against the Inductive Miner (RQ3), embedding quality against baselines (RQ1, RQ3), and cross-modal retrieval (RQ1). All results are computed on the held-out test split of $1{,}024$ triples ($512$ behavior families)---used neither for optimization nor for model selection---with the final checkpoint after early stopping and deterministic latent means.

\subsection{Training Behavior}
\label{sec:trainingbehavior}



Figure~\ref{fig:training} shows the training run. Training converged smoothly: the validation loss decreased from $2.50$ to a best value of $1.905$, the schedule halved the learning rate once (epoch 31), and early stopping ended the run after epoch 32; the best-validation checkpoint is epoch 28, while the final checkpoint is used for the test report below. The validation loss is lower than the training loss because training uses dropout, stochastic latent sampling, grouped positives, and activity remapping, whereas validation and testing use deterministic latent means and unremapped inputs. All validation loss components improve together (Figure~\ref{fig:training}b): reconstruction is slightly easier than the two translation tasks, and the contrastive loss drops from $1.70$ to $1.31$. The final-checkpoint test loss is $1.416$ (tree reconstruction $0.428$, trace$\to$tree $0.450$, Petri$\to$tree $0.436$, contrastive $0.800$, KL $19.903$). Training takes about 46 minutes on a single CPU; no GPU is required at this scale.

The semantic half is also strong. The decoded tree \emph{exactly} equals the canonical target in 85.9\% of the cases when decoding from the tree's own embedding, 85.2\% from the log, and 85.7\% from the Petri-net structure. Exact match is demanding (a single differing leaf counts as failure); the error-tolerant metrics are more informative: the normalized token edit distance is low (0.063--0.083), and the \emph{behavioral} distance between the original log and traces simulated from the decoded model ranges from 0.310 to 0.336, whereas the same distance between two \emph{unrelated} test logs averages 1.209 (Section~\ref{sec:embeddingresults})---decoded models are behaviorally much closer to their source than a random model of the same distribution. The ordering of sources is now nearly tied, indicating that tree, log, and Petri encoders all retain enough information for high-quality tree decoding.

The decoder retains the two upper exclusive-choice layers and one parallel block, but reduces a 23-node, depth-9 source to 13 nodes and depth 5, replacing its deep sequence--parallel--loop continuation with a shallow choice. Thus grammar masking still guarantees a valid model, but does not prevent semantic simplification near the upper end of the learned complexity distribution.

\subsection{Process-Discovery Quality (RQ3)}
\label{sec:discoveryresults}



Table~\ref{tab:discovery} addresses the log-to-model use case: each test log is translated by ProcRosetta (encode the log, decode a tree, convert to a net) and, independently, mined with PM4Py's Inductive Miner; both models are scored against the log with alignment-based fitness~\cite{Adriansyah2012} and precision.

The Inductive Miner achieves perfect fitness (1.000) and an F1 of 0.980. This is expected: the test logs are complete, noise-free sampled views of structured behavior---precisely the setting in which the Inductive Miner enjoys rediscovery guarantees on block-structured logs~\cite{Leemans2013}---so the comparison is intentionally played on its home turf. ProcRosetta reaches a fitness of 0.950, a precision of 0.937, and an F1 of 0.938---about 96\% of the Inductive Miner's F1. Notably, a purely neural translation---the entire log is compressed into a single 256-dimensional vector, with no search, replay, or counting---lands within this range while producing a valid model for every log. The remaining gap is concentrated in fitness: a slightly wrong decoded tree can make some observed traces non-replayable, whereas the Inductive Miner is designed to include them. We interpret this not as a claim that neural translation should replace classical discovery, but as evidence that the shared latent space preserves enough behavioral information to support discovery-grade model generation.

Figure~\ref{fig:discoveryexamples} provides an out-of-distribution comparison on two supplied event logs. The ProcRosetta trees are reconstructed directly from the two tool screenshots; no activity label is abbreviated (long labels are only line-wrapped). For a common reference, both models are scored on the same frequency-preserving input sample: 128 traces for \texttt{receipt.xes} and all 100 traces for \texttt{roadtraffic100traces.xes}. On the receipt log, the 16-node screenshot tree retains 10 activities and reaches alignment F1 0.193, compared with 0.483 for the Inductive Miner's 47-node tree. On road traffic, the 16-node screenshot tree contains all ten activities but reaches F1 0.202, whereas the Inductive Miner reaches 0.850. The two ProcRosetta outputs share a compact top-level-choice and nested-loop structure, but the low precision (0.125 in both cases) shows that arranging the labels into a valid tree is not enough to capture their ordering constraints. The deterministic miner fits the observed variants much more closely, so these examples support the aggregate in-distribution result without suggesting equivalent performance on external logs.

\subsection{Embedding Quality (RQ1, RQ3)}
\label{sec:embeddingresults}

If the latent space is a space of process \emph{behavior}, then distances in it should correlate with behavioral distances. The assessment quantifies the behavioral distance between two logs as the mean of three $L_1$ distances between empirical distributions extracted from the logs---trace variants, directly-follows relations (with boundary symbols)~\cite{vanDerAalst2019DFG}, and trace lengths---each in $[0,2]$. Across all $523{,}776$ pairs of test logs, this distance averages $1.209$ (min $0.0$, max $1.987$). Each embedding method is scored by: (i)~the Spearman rank correlation $\rho$ between embedding cosine distance and behavioral distance over all pairs; (ii)~the mean behavioral distance from each sample to its \emph{nearest neighbor} in the embedding (NN behavior; lower is better); and (iii)~the improvement of the latter over the random-pair average (higher is better).

\subsection{Cross-Modal Retrieval (RQ1)}
\label{sec:retrievalresults}



The most direct test of cross-modal alignment is retrieval: given, e.g., a Petri net, does its embedding find the matching event log row among all 1{,}024 test rows? Table~\ref{tab:retrieval} reports top-1 accuracy, mean reciprocal rank (MRR), and the mean rank of the true counterpart, for all six directions. All directions perform above chance: top-1 accuracies of 2.0--6.3\% against a 0.1\% chance level (factors of about 20--65), and mean ranks of about 101--102 against an expected 512 for random ranking. These exact-row scores are deliberately strict in the behavior-family setting: each query has one indexed target row, but equivalent alternate rows may be behaviorally just as correct. The separate family-equivalence check in the test report confirms very high within-family cosine similarity (0.995--1.000), with especially strong alternate-representation retrieval for logs. The results establish that the shared space aligns modalities above chance at instance level, while leaving clear headroom for exact cross-modal retrieval.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Implementation}
\label{sec:repository}

ProcRosetta is implemented in Python on top of PyTorch and PM4Py~\cite{Berti2023PM4Py}; the complete source code (synthetic data generator, training and evaluation pipelines, and all metric implementations) is publicly available at \url{https://github.com/fit-alessandro-berti/proc-rosetta}. Three commands reproduce the study end-to-end: \texttt{sample.py} regenerates the seeded splits of Table~\ref{tab:dataset}, \texttt{train.py} trains the model of Table~\ref{tab:hyper} and writes the per-epoch metrics of Figure~\ref{fig:training}, and \texttt{test.py} produces every number in Tables~\ref{tab:decode}--\ref{tab:retrieval}. The repository also provides utilities that apply a trained checkpoint to external artifacts (\texttt{.xes} event logs, \texttt{.pnml} Petri nets, \texttt{.ptml} process trees), computing embeddings and decoding logs or nets into process trees with automatic canonicalization and label restoration.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Threats to validity.}
Four threats qualify these findings. \emph{(i) Synthetic data.} Training and evaluation use synthetic behavior families with controlled alternate Petri-net representations; real logs exhibit noise, incompleteness, deviating behavior, and long-tail variants absent from this distribution, so the conclusions concern in-distribution artifacts (out-of-distribution quality is not claimed here). \emph{(ii) Metric circularity.} The behavioral distance used for embedding evaluation shares two of its three components with the directly-follows and trace-variant baselines, inflating their scores; Table~\ref{tab:embedding} marks them, and the less affected nearest-neighbor quality is reported alongside correlation. \emph{(iii) Duplicate behavior across splits.} Splits are family-wise, but the generator does not explicitly deduplicate visible languages across splits, so a fraction of test behaviors may resemble training behaviors; the exact-match rates of Table~\ref{tab:decode} should be read with this in mind. \emph{(iv) Single configuration.} All results come from one seeded data configuration and one trained model; variance across seeds and hyperparameter sensitivity are not quantified.

\paragraph{Scope of the model class.}
The canonical target trees used in the experiments cover sequence, exclusive choice, and concurrency; the grammar, tokenizer, and decoder already support the loop operator, so extending the trained model to loops is a data-configuration change rather than an architectural one. Some Petri inputs are non-block or non-free-choice variants, but the decoder still emits process trees. More fundamentally, the process-tree target restricts outputs to block-structured behavior---the restriction the Inductive Miner accepts in exchange for soundness---and is less expressive than partially ordered formalisms such as POWL~\cite{Kourani2023POWL,Kourani2026}. The architecture is modular here: any target language with a prefix grammar, a canonical form, and a deterministic conversion to Petri nets could replace the process-tree decoder.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper introduced ProcRosetta, a multimodal neural framework that embeds the three central artifact types of process mining---event logs, process trees, and Petri nets---into a single latent space of process behavior and decodes any point of that space into a grammar-valid process tree that converts deterministically into a sound Petri net. The assessment answered all three research questions affirmatively and quantified the remaining headroom: the three encoders converge to one shared geometry that aligns paired artifacts above chance at exact-row level and strongly at behavior-family level (RQ1); grammar-masked decoding produced a valid, Petri-convertible process tree in 100\% of 4{,}096 test decodes, with decoded behavior far closer to the source than unrelated models (RQ2); and the learned 256-dimensional embeddings rival much higher-dimensional deterministic features while log-to-model translation reaches about 96\% of the Inductive Miner's F1 in the miner's own best-case setting (RQ3).

Future work follows directly from the assessment. On the data side: larger and more diverse tree distributions including loops, noisy and incomplete simulated logs, quantified near-duplicate behavior across splits, and validation on real-life logs and model collections. On the model side: stronger cross-modal retrieval objectives, beam or sampling-based decoding, multi-seed robustness studies, and scaling the encoders. On the representation side: richer sound target languages such as POWL~\cite{Kourani2026} as decoder output, an exact-conformance-based evaluation distance, and attaching further encoders---including natural-language process descriptions~\cite{Kourani2024LLM}---to the same latent space, moving closer to a true Rosetta stone for process representations.
