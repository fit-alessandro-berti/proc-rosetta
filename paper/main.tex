\abstract{Process mining represents behavior through event logs, process trees, and Petri nets, yet learning systems usually handle these structures separately. We introduce \model, a behavior-supervised multimodal model that embeds all three artifacts in a shared space and translates any supported input into a process tree. Training groups one canonical tree, alternative Petri-net realizations, and finite log observations into behavior families, while recording which visible-trace and partial-order properties each pair shares. On held-out synthetic families from the same generator design, the shared space supports cross-modal retrieval and follows an operational behavioral ordering. Grammar-constrained decoding always returns a well-formed, executable block-structured tree, but often fails to preserve behavior, particularly from event logs, and gives lower conformance than specialized discovery. \model is therefore best used as a representation and retrieval layer whose translated candidates are validated with established process-mining methods; the real-log cases are illustrative rather than evidence of industrial transfer.}

\keywords{process mining, multimodal representation learning, behavior supervision, process trees, Petri nets, event logs, grammar-constrained decoding}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Process mining uses different artifacts to describe related behavior. Event logs record observed executions. Process trees organize choices, sequences, loops, and parallel work into a readable hierarchy. Petri nets express states, concurrency, and routing directly. These views are connected, but their structures are too different for a representation learned on one view to be used immediately on another.

Figure~\ref{fig:intuitive-example} illustrates the opportunity. A small process can appear as a log, a tree, or a marked net. A shared representation should retrieve the corresponding artifacts across these forms and provide a common starting point for translation. The task is not simple format conversion: a finite log is incomplete, and structurally different models can allow the same observed traces.

\input{figures/intuitive-example}

We introduce \model to learn such a shared representation for event logs, process trees, and Petri nets. Modality-specific encoders map the artifacts into one semantic space, while a grammar-constrained decoder returns a block-structured process tree. Training is organized around \emph{behavior families} that pair alternative model realizations with different log observations and record which behavioral properties they share.

The evaluation is deliberately scoped to family-unseen synthetic data and process-tree outputs; Sections~\ref{sec:experiment} and~\ref{sec:threats} state the resulting generalization and expressivity limits. Within this scope, the paper asks:

\begin{description}
\item[\RQone:] Does the shared space align related artifacts and preserve behavioral ordering?
\item[\RQtwo:] How much semantic information is preserved when decoding a valid process tree?
\item[\RQthree:] Does the representation support retrieval, and how does log-to-tree decoding compare with specialized discovery?
\end{description}

The central claim is correspondingly focused. Behavior-family supervision produces a useful cross-artifact representation, whereas constrained decoding guarantees syntax but not semantic fidelity. The paper contributes the family-based supervision scheme, a multimodal architecture, an RQ-aligned evaluation, and released experimental artifacts that expose both outcomes.

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Process representations and discovery}

An event log records completed executions but only partially samples the behavior of an underlying process. A process tree composes activities and silent steps through sequence, exclusive choice, parallelism, and loop operators. Its block structure permits conversion into sound workflow nets. A Petri net expresses places, transitions, arcs, and markings directly and can represent non-block structures not expressible by the chosen tree grammar \citep{vanDerAalst2016,Kourani2026}.

Classical process discovery constructs a model from a log. Inductive Miner recursively detects cuts and returns a sound block-structured result \citep{Leemans2013}. We use the PM4Py implementation for conversion, simulation, and evaluation \citep{Berti2023PM4Py}. Learned discovery instead estimates a model from examples; graph-neural discovery is one relevant direction \citep{Sommers2023}. Our task is broader than discovery: the same parameters encode three artifact types, support retrieval and geometry, and decode all of them into one exchange language. Inductive Miner is therefore the relevant specialized discovery baseline.

Conformance combines several perspectives. Fitness measures whether observed traces can be replayed, while precision penalizes model behavior unsupported by the log \citep{Adriansyah2013}. Directly-follows summaries are computationally convenient but lose long-range and concurrency information \citep{vanDerAalst2019DFG}. We therefore report both distributional behavioral distances and process-model conformance, and avoid treating any single metric as semantics in full.

\subsection{Representation learning for process mining}

Trace-encoding methods range from handcrafted features to sequence models; their assumptions and performance vary considerably by task \citep{Barbon2023}. Earlier work learned representations at activity, trace, log, and model levels \citep{DeKoninck2018}. Petri-net embeddings have also been learned from graph structure \citep{Colonna2024}, and graph distances can be compared with model-similarity measures \citep{Dijkman2011}. These approaches establish the value of vector representations but generally do not align raw logs, hierarchical trees, and marked Petri graphs in one behavior-supervised space.

A concurrent direction uses in-context learning on event logs for predictive monitoring \citep{Berti2026ICFM}. That setting centers on one input modality and prediction over cases. \model instead aligns multiple artifact types and produces complete structured models. The two directions are complementary: a future system could combine event-level monitoring capabilities with artifact-level semantic translation.

\subsection{Multimodal alignment and constrained generation}

Contrastive multimodal learning demonstrates that paired observations can define a shared space without requiring identical encoders \citep{Radford2021}. Supervised contrastive objectives allow all members of a semantic class to act as positives \citep{Khosla2020}. Our classes are behavior families rather than image--text pairs, and the separately recorded visible-language and partial-order semantics determine which pairs may be used as strong positives. Variance and covariance regularization reduce collapse without imposing a probabilistic prior \citep{Bardes2022}.

Transformers provide the attention mechanism used in the process-tree encoder, set-attention component, and decoder \citep{Vaswani2017}. The event-log encoder must additionally be invariant to trace order, motivating set attention \citep{Lee2019}. For output, grammar-constrained decoding can guarantee syntactic structure even when model probabilities are imperfect \citep{Geng2023}. Our decoder extends this principle with explicit operator arities, source-activity masking, contextual copy scores, and deterministic completion when the search budget is exhausted.

\section{Problem formulation and research questions}\label{sec:setting}

Training examples are generated as families rather than as independent triples. Consider the duplicate-prefix behavior
\begin{equation}
T=\mathsf{XOR}\bigl(
  \mathsf{SEQ}(\textsf{register},\textsf{accept}),
  \mathsf{SEQ}(\textsf{register},\textsf{reject})
\bigr).
\label{eq:worked-family}
\end{equation}
One Petri-net realization, $N^{(1)}$, has a distinct transition labeled \textsf{register} on each choice branch. A second realization, $N^{(2)}$, uses one shared \textsf{register} transition followed by two silent routing transitions. Their internal structures differ, but both allow exactly
\[
\langle\textsf{register},\textsf{accept}\rangle
\quad\text{and}\quad
\langle\textsf{register},\textsf{reject}\rangle.
\]
The generator creates two finite logs from this common trace pool. The cycled-coverage view repeats available variants evenly before shuffling, whereas the resampled view draws variants independently with replacement. Pairing each log with each realization gives the four observations in Table~\ref{tab:worked-family}.

These rows are four observations of one behavior family, not four independently sampled behaviors. The family is both the unit of supervision and the unit assigned to a data split. We now formalize the artifact types and tasks illustrated by this example.

Let $\mathcal{A}$ be a finite activity alphabet and $\tauact$ an invisible activity. A trace is a word $\sigma\in\mathcal{A}^{*}$, and an event log $L$ is a finite multiset of traces. A process tree $T$ is generated by
\begin{equation}
T ::= a \mid \tauact \mid \mathsf{SEQ}_{k}(T_1,\ldots,T_k)
 \mid \mathsf{XOR}_{k}(T_1,\ldots,T_k)
 \mid \mathsf{AND}_{k}(T_1,\ldots,T_k)
 \mid \mathsf{LOOP}(T_1,T_2),
\label{eq:grammar}
\end{equation}
where $a\in\mathcal{A}$ and $k\geq2$. A labeled Petri net is a tuple $N=(P,U,F,\ell,m_0,m_f)$ with places $P$, transitions $U$, flow relation $F$, labels $\ell:U\rightarrow\mathcal{A}\cup\{\tauact\}$, and initial and final markings $m_0,m_f$. We use a process tree as the canonical exchange representation because its compact grammar supports constrained generation and conversion into a sound workflow net. This makes supervised decoding possible, but limits the output to block-structured behavior; Petri nets can express structures outside that grammar.

A \emph{behavior family} $f$ contains a canonical tree $T_f$, two Petri-net realizations $N_f^{(1)},N_f^{(2)}$, and two event-log observations $L_f^{(u)},L_f^{(r)}$, where $u$ denotes the cycled-coverage implementation mode and $r$ the independent resample. Their pairing is
\[
\begin{array}{c|cc}
 & L_f^{(u)} & L_f^{(r)}\\
\hline
N_f^{(1)} & (T_f,N_f^{(1)},L_f^{(u)}) & (T_f,N_f^{(1)},L_f^{(r)})\\
N_f^{(2)} & (T_f,N_f^{(2)},L_f^{(u)}) & (T_f,N_f^{(2)},L_f^{(r)})
\end{array}
\]
Thus one family produces four aligned rows. The same underlying tree is paired with every row; each log observation and Petri-net realization participates in two rows.

For modality $m\in\{T,L,N\}$, an encoder produces two outputs. Contextual source memory $H_m$ retains local information used by the decoder, while a compact normalized vector $\vect z_m$ summarizes the artifact for retrieval and comparison. The shared-embedding task places related family members near one another despite their different structures. Cross-modal retrieval ranks candidate artifacts by cosine similarity between these vectors. Direct translation conditions a decoder on $H_m$ and returns a tree in Eq.~\eqref{eq:grammar}. For optional fusion, the available semantic vectors are averaged,
\begin{equation}
\vect z_{\mathrm{fuse}}=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{m\in S}\vect z_m,
\qquad S\subseteq\{T,L,N\},
\label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
and projected into decoder memory. Translation is therefore always into a process tree, even when the source is a Petri net. These tasks instantiate \RQone--\RQthree{} from the Introduction.

\section{The ProcRosetta method}\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Behavior families as the supervision unit}\label{sec:corpus}

The multiplicity inside a family has a distinct purpose. Two Petri-net realizations teach that internal place names, transition identifiers, silent routing, and one serialization are not themselves the behavior. Isomorphic copies provide an easy instance of this invariance; controlled families add structurally different realizations so that graph correspondence alone is insufficient. Two event-log observations teach that a finite sample and its empirical frequencies are not the complete process. One canonical tree gives the decoder a unique supervised target even though several models may be compatible with the observations.

Starting from one behavior, the generator performs six steps:
\begin{enumerate}
\item choose an ordinary random family or a controlled anchor motif;
\item construct the canonical process tree;
\item construct two Petri-net realizations;
\item record visible-language evidence and partial-order information;
\item construct a trace pool and two finite log observations; and
\item relabel each observation consistently across modalities and expand the family into four rows.
\end{enumerate}
Figure~\ref{fig:corpus} groups these steps into four visual stages. Family membership is fixed before the final Cartesian product; Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:row-expansion} makes the view-specific relabeling and four-row expansion explicit.

\input{figures/corpus}

All four rows from a family are assigned to the same split. Thus neither the two log observations nor the two Petri-net realizations of one generated family can cross a train--test boundary. The generator does not globally deduplicate canonical trees or complete visible languages across independently sampled families, however. The experiment therefore measures generalization to unseen generated families, not strict behavior-disjoint or generator-shift generalization.

\subsection{Ordinary random families and controlled anchor families}

Ordinary families are generated topology first. The sampler plans a root operator, recursively samples an unlabeled topology, and rejects a topology that cannot meet the required number of distinct activities. It then assigns distinct labels until that floor is met, applies label reuse only to later leaves, canonicalizes XOR and AND child order, folds the tree, and measures the target that the decoder will actually see. A candidate is accepted only if this folded target meets the ordinary profile; otherwise sampling restarts, and exhausting 4,000 attempts raises an error rather than relaxing the bounds. No artificial sequence padding is inserted to satisfy an activity floor. Appendix Fig.~\ref{fig:tree-sampler} visualizes this acceptance loop.

Controlled families instead place one of three small contrasts inside the same randomized context for both realizations. These contexts contain 4--12 nodes and are generated separately from the ordinary size, depth, and activity filters. The duplicate-prefix, concurrency/interleaving, and non-free-choice M-pattern anchors remain present at every complexity level: the anchors provide stable representation contrasts while the ordinary random trees become larger and deeper. The trace pool is constructed before either log view. For finite non-looping families, the generator enumerates the complete visible traces of both Petri-net realizations, verifies equality of the collected sets, and marks the result exact only when both searches terminate. An ordinary looping tree may have an infinite language, so its pair is certified by construction isomorphism instead; 512 seeded tree playouts are simulated, deduplicated, and sorted to form a finite pool.

Both stored log observations are then drawn from this pool. The \emph{cycled-coverage view} (implementation mode \texttt{uniform\_variants}) cycles through the deterministic pool until 128 traces have been selected and then shuffles them with a derived seed. If the pool has more than 128 variants, only its first 128 sorted variants enter this view. The \emph{independent resample} draws 128 traces with replacement. For example, from $\{ab,ac,ad\}$ a six-trace coverage view contains two copies of each variant before shuffling, whereas a resample might be $ab,ab,ac,ab,ad,ac$. Coverage-view frequencies are a sampling device, not an estimated operational distribution. Persisted logs remain clean so evaluation is not confounded by unknown corruption; incomplete and noisy presentations are generated only as training-time augmentation for observation consistency.

Finally, each log view receives its own first-seen activity map. If one worked-example view begins with \textsf{register}, \textsf{accept}, then \textsf{register} becomes $A_0$, \textsf{accept} becomes $A_1$, and \textsf{reject} becomes $A_2$. If the other begins with \textsf{register}, \textsf{reject}, the last two canonical names are reversed. Family labels absent from the finite view are appended in stable tree/trace-pool order. The view-specific map is applied consistently to its tree, every trace, and all visible Petri-net transitions. Literal labels and first-appearance order therefore cannot identify a family; the stored behavior and partial-order identifiers determine positive pairs.

\subsection{Generator implementation and relation checks}\label{sec:generation}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:generation} summarizes the implementation after the preceding narrative. A global seed is expanded with BLAKE2b into family-specific streams for the process model, representation, playout, log view, and augmentation, so structural and observation sampling are independent.

\begin{algorithm*}[!t]
\centering
\caption{Behavior-family generation}
\label{alg:generation}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require complexity profile $c$, family target $n$, deterministic seed $s$
\State initialize split-local family list $\mathcal{F}\leftarrow\varnothing$
\State allocate controlled-motif and ordinary-root quotas
\While{$|\mathcal{F}|<n$}
  \State derive family-specific random streams from $s$
  \State sample an ordinary tree under $c$ or place a controlled motif in a shared context
  \State construct paired Petri-net realizations $N^{(1)},N^{(2)}$
  \State reject failed conversion, reachability, or dead-transition checks
  \If{$T$ is an ordinary looping tree}
    \State record construction isomorphism and simulate a seeded trace pool
  \Else
    \State enumerate each visible language under the configured safeguards
    \State reject empty or unequal collected languages
    \State record exact evidence if both searches finish; bounded evidence otherwise
  \EndIf
  \State construct cycled-coverage and independently resampled log views
  \State apply each first-seen map and flatten two model variants by two log views
  \State store relation metadata, targets, seeds, and four rows
\EndWhile
\State verify coverage constraints and commit the split
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm*}

For non-looping families, marking exploration stops after 5,000 visited states, 10,000 completed traces, visible length 32, or the silent-transition firing safeguard. Reaching one of these limits records only bounded evidence; such families may remain in held-out splits but are excluded from training by the resolved exact-only policy. The generator also rejects unreachable final markings and dead transitions, verifies motif and representation-slot coverage, and stores the relation certificate in every row rather than reconstructing it later from surface similarity.

The shared decoder predicts tree structure, operator arity, and visible activities. Its copy component can reuse activities represented in source memory. During generation, a small automaton tracks open child slots and masks illegal tokens. End-of-sequence is available only when every slot is filled; if the search budget is exhausted, deterministic completion closes the remaining slots. The decoder therefore guarantees a well-formed tree within the supported grammar, but this guarantee says nothing about behavioral accuracy. The evaluated synthetic policy restricts activities to the source alphabet and suppresses repeated visible labels. Because repeated labels can be legitimate, the external-log cases also evaluate the same latent with that mask disabled.

These signals form three conceptual groups: reconstruction from single modalities and fusion; alignment through strong-pair contrast, relation hierarchy, observation consistency, and operational geometry; and regularization for latent diversity and stable structured generation. \begin{align}
\mathcal{L}={}&
 \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}
 +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{fuse}}
 +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{exact}}
 +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hier}}
 +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{obs}}
 +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{geom}}
 +\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}.
\label{eq:objective}

The four principal reconstruction weights are 1.00; exact cross-modal contrast is weighted 0.30, hierarchical ordering and observation consistency 0.15 each, and soft behavioral geometry 0.25. The semantic latent is deterministic, so the Kullback--Leibler coefficient is zero rather than a probabilistic prior.

\subsection{Curriculum learning and checkpoint selection}\label{sec:curriculum}

We distinguish a \emph{complexity profile} (the ordinary-tree generator bounds), a \emph{complexity corpus} or level (an independently generated simple, medium, or complex dataset), and a \emph{curriculum stage} (the batch mixture currently used for training). Curriculum learning is the competence-gated progression between these mixtures.

The progression separates several coupled tasks. The model must align three modalities, identify activities consistently, and generate a valid tree prefix autoregressively. Beginning with trees of up to 80 nodes makes these skills difficult to acquire together: an incorrect operator or arity changes the number of open child positions and can affect every later token. The simple stage therefore emphasizes grammar closure, activity copying, and initial alignment on short structures. Later stages introduce deeper nesting, more labels, and longer-range dependencies. Easier rehearsal batches remain active to reduce forgetting, while the controlled motifs remain at every level as stable representation anchors.

\subsection{Fixed evaluation subset}

We evaluate 16 families from each of the three complexity levels, for 48 families in total; each family contributes four aligned rows (192 rows and 768 decoding runs across four source conditions). Families are chosen deterministically with interleaving across complexity, motif, and observation strata. Results refer to this subset unless explicitly described as corpus statistics.

For decoded-model comparisons, tree-edit distance is normalized by the larger folded-tree size. The operational behavioral distance between families is
\begin{equation}
d_B(f,g)=\frac{1}{3}\left(
 \lVert p_{\mathrm{var}}^f-p_{\mathrm{var}}^g\rVert_1+
 \lVert p_{\mathrm{df}}^f-p_{\mathrm{df}}^g\rVert_1+
 \lVert p_{\mathrm{len}}^f-p_{\mathrm{len}}^g\rVert_1
\right),
\label{eq:behavior-distance}
\end{equation}
where the terms compare trace-variant, boundary-aware directly-follows, and trace-length distributions. This metric overlaps with statistics used by the training-time behavioral descriptor. Agreement with $d_B$ is therefore evidence about the supervised operational notion of behavior, not a general process-semantic metric.

Uncertainty is estimated by resampling complete families, never individual rows, with replacement \citep{Efron1994}. Percentile intervals summarize variation within the fixed subset; they do not include generator, training-seed, or checkpoint uncertainty.

MRR can also depend on deterministic tie order and is treated as secondary. The discovery comparison evaluates log-decoded trees and Inductive Miner using footprint fitness and precision.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{\RQone: Why multimodal alignment succeeds}

Behavior families provide a supervision unit above surface syntax. Alternate event-log observations teach invariance to sampling, while paired Petri-net realizations teach invariance to representation. Separating certificate mechanism, visible-language identity, and partial-order identity prevents a useful structural contrast from becoming a false identity. The result is a space that can support neighborhoods and retrieval even when the encoders share no input format.

The behavioral-ordering result is narrower. Its target is an operational descriptor related to training supervision, and deterministic features remain competitive. The contribution is therefore the availability of one learned interface from every modality, not the replacement of all modality-specific similarity measures.

\subsection{\RQtwo: Why semantic decoding remains difficult}

Alignment requires related artifacts to occupy compatible neighborhoods; decoding requires a long discrete structure to be recovered from an incomplete or compressed representation. A finite event log can support several plausible trees, while training designates one canonical target. An early structural error also changes all later open slots in prefix generation, so local mistakes propagate.

The copy mechanism controls the activity vocabulary, and the grammar controls well-formedness, but neither chooses the correct control-flow placement. Better generation will require objectives that score complete behavior, stronger size priors, or conformance-aware reranking. A richer output language would also be needed for Petri nets that have no equivalent block-structured tree.

The duplicate-label comparison isolates one possible source of error. Disabling the mask does not change the top candidate for either real log, so their low conformance cannot be attributed to forced label uniqueness. In these cases the decoder instead omits activities and compresses control flow too aggressively.

\subsection{\RQthree: Appropriate and inappropriate uses}

The current model is appropriate for encoding heterogeneous artifacts, retrieving related material, comparing neighborhoods, and proposing process-tree candidates for validation. Direct translation uses source memory, retrieval uses semantic vectors, and exploratory fusion combines semantic vectors before decoding. These uses are available through the command-line and browser interfaces; Section~\ref{sec:reproduction} gives the operational commands used in the paper.

The model is not an unattended replacement for process discovery or conformance checking. A practical workflow should retrieve context or propose candidates with \model, then validate behavior with established process-mining methods. Fusion is a transparent common interface but does not yet improve reconstruction, suggesting that future fusion should account for source reliability.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Limitations}\label{sec:threats}

\paragraph{Data and generalization.}
The limitation is that training and inferential evaluation use synthetic families from the same generator design, without timestamps, resources, lifecycle attributes, drift, or label semantics. The real-log evidence covers only two available logs, one of them a small extract, and neither comes with aligned reference trees and Petri nets. Families are split without row leakage, but the synthetic splits are not guaranteed to be behavior-disjoint, and the process-tree output language cannot express every Petri net. Consequently, the results do not establish industrial transfer or general Petri-net translation. The appropriate mitigation is a multi-artifact benchmark with real processes, generator shift, global behavior deduplication, and explicit coverage of non-block structures.

\paragraph{Evaluation and uncertainty.}
The limitation is a fixed subset, one training seed, finite behavioral sampling, and conditional evaluability for some large outputs. In addition, $d_B$ overlaps the training descriptor. Consequently, the intervals describe family variation inside this experiment rather than training or domain uncertainty, and geometry should not be read as general semantic equivalence. Larger family-complete evaluations, repeated training runs, independent conformance measures, and explicit failure accounting are needed.

\paragraph{Comparative evidence.}
The limitation is that the study compares with deterministic representation baselines and Inductive Miner but does not include every discovery method, a directly comparable learned multimodal system, or retrained component ablations. Software-version sensitivity also affects simulation and conformance. Consequently, the experiment establishes system-level behavior but not the causal necessity of every architecture or loss term. Retrained ablations, broader baselines, locked environments, and independent replications would strengthen the evidence.

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

\model aligns event logs, process trees, and Petri nets through behavior-family supervision and a shared semantic interface. The representation supports cross-artifact retrieval and operational behavioral ordering, while grammar-constrained translation remains semantically unreliable despite guaranteed syntax. Illustrative real-log cases confirm the discovery gap and show that allowing repeated labels alone does not repair it. The model is therefore best used to retrieve related artifacts and propose candidates that are subsequently validated. The most important next experiment is evaluation on a behavior-disjoint multi-artifact benchmark containing real processes and structures outside the process-tree grammar.
